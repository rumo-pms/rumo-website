34|Getting <em>started</em>
35|The first week. Logging in, setting up rooms, booking your first reservation, connecting your first OTA. No two-hour onboarding call. Just do it, and ring us if you get stuck.
36|First login
37|Logging in to rumo for the first time
38|You get a mail from us with a personal link. Click, choose a password, done. No separate hotel ID, no "ask your administrator". If you are the owner yourself, you are the administrator too.
39|<em>Do not forget:</em> a second factor is a setting of the hotel, not of your own account. A new hotel starts with it required; a hotel from before that default keeps whatever it had. With it on, anyone who does not have one yet is asked for it at their next sign-in.
40|Setup
41|<em>Setting up</em> your rooms and room types
42|Room types first (Standard, Deluxe, Junior Suite, …), then individual rooms. A room type has a rate and a maximum occupancy; an individual room hangs on such a type. The same pattern Booking.com and the other OTAs expect.
43|Tip: keep room types <strong>limited</strong>. Three or four is usually enough. Twenty room types gives you twenty synchronisation problems.
44|Demo data
45|Playing with made-up reservations
46|A trial does not start empty but as a finished demo hotel: a few room types, a few rates and a few weeks of bookings on the calendar, so there is something to look at from your first sign-in. Every one of those bookings carries the label <em>Demo</em>; a reservation you make yourself never does, so you cannot mix them up.
47|There is no button that empties everything in one go. Your own rooms and rates come later, in the conversation with Rudy, at the moment you really start.
48|Migration
49|Coming from another PMS
50|You pull an export out of your current system and read it into your own rumo. Existing reservations, guest profiles and your history come along, and your old system keeps running in the meantime until you switch over. So your file stays with you: we need to receive nothing of it. Most hotels are fully across within a few days, without double entry.
51|Coming from a dog-eared notebook? That is fine too: then you start with an empty calendar and put the coming bookings in. Just ask and we will go through it together.

53|Reservations &amp; <em>guest portal</em>
54|The part you are in every day. Creating, changing, checking in and out, invoicing. Plus the guest portal: the link your guest gets to fill in their own pre-check-in.
55|Creating
56|Creating a reservation at the <em>desk</em>
57|Walk-in, phone, mail: press <strong>+ New</strong> or drag an empty strip on the calendar. Choose room type, dates, number of people. rumo pulls the modifier price for that period automatically; you can adjust it. Guest details can follow later through the pre-check-in link, or straight away by typing them in.
58|For a walk-in: tick <em>"check in immediately"</em> and the guest is in-house at once and the room status jumps to <strong>occupied</strong>.
59|Daily
60|Checking in and <em>checking out</em>
61|<strong>Checking in:</strong> open today's arrivals list. Click a reservation open, check whether the pre-check-in is complete, assign a room if that has not happened yet, status to <em>checked in</em>. Done.
62|<strong>Checking out:</strong> open the departures list, add the outstanding items (minibar, breakfasts, extras), take payment, the invoice follows automatically. If something is still open, rumo warns you, but it does not hold you back: checking out with an outstanding balance is allowed, on an invoice to a company for instance.
63|Groups
64|A group of several <em>rooms at once</em>
65|Create one main reservation and add several rooms to it. All under the same booking reference. Dates can differ per room (a wedding where the couple stays one night longer, for instance).
66|For invoicing you choose: one collective invoice in the organiser's name, or separate invoices per room or guest. Both are possible, without having to enter the booking again.
67|Changing
68|Changing, splitting or <em>moving</em> a reservation
69|Shifting dates or swapping a room is simply a matter of dragging on the calendar. Drag between rooms within the same type, or between types. rumo recalculates the price and proposes a new line. You decide whether it may go through.
70|Cost items (minibar, breakfasts, extras) can be <strong>moved between reservations</strong> by searching for a booking reference. No date filter, no restrictions: just find the right reservation and drag the line.
71|Identity
72|<em>Scanning</em> an ID or passport at check-in
73|If the guest did not fill in their details beforehand, you scan their identity card or passport at the desk and name, date of birth and nationality land in the file straight away.
74|That is the same input as the one from the guest portal, so it also feeds the police registration and the government statistics. Typed once, used three times.
75|Online check-in
76|The guest portal for your guest
77|Every guest gets a personal link in the confirmation mail. With it they fill in themselves:
78|Pre-check-in details (ID, address, date of birth)
79|Confirming or declining a quote
80|Paying online or leaving a card as a guarantee
81|The link is valid <em>until 30 days after check-out</em>, in case a guest still has to add something or wants to download the invoice.
82|Cancellations
83|<em>Cancelling</em> a reservation: direct vs. OTA
84|<strong>Booked directly:</strong> cancel in rumo. You decide yourself whether a cancellation fee is charged and whether a credit note is made.
85|<strong>Booked through an OTA:</strong> the cancellation has to happen at the OTA (Booking.com, Airbnb, Expedia). rumo synchronises the status automatically as soon as the OTA passes it on. That is a choice by the OTAs, not a shortcoming of rumo. They do not let PMSs write their cancellations back directly.
86|Quotes
87|Sending a quote and having it confirmed
88|Create a reservation with status <em>option</em>, give it an expiry date, and send the guest a link through the guest portal. The guest confirms online and possibly pays right away. Only then does it become a real reservation. No more yes/no/maybe mails.
89|Calendar
90|What you can do on the <em>calendar</em>
91|The calendar shows your rooms one under the other and the days alongside. You choose whether you look at one day, a week or a date range of your own.
92|Dragging does three things, depending on where to: pulling at the edge extends or shortens a stay, moving to another row moves the booking to another room, and left or right shifts the dates. Every booking also has a pair of scissors to cut a stay in two.
93|The colours stand for the room status: in house, clean, still to be cleaned, blocked. Click a name and you are in the guest file.
94|Full but you do not want to lose the request? Then you put it on the waiting list instead of refusing it.
95|No-show
96|What to do with a no-show
97|You mark the reservation as a no-show. rumo keeps the room day in the occupancy statistics (so your revenue is right) and proposes charging the standard no-show fee. Or not: that stays your choice, not the system's.
98|Guests &amp; <em>companies</em>
99|Who has stayed before, which company gets the invoice, and which rate goes with it. The file behind your bookings.
100|Three kinds
101|Guest, booker or <em>company</em>
102|Three tabs, and the difference matters. The <strong>guest</strong> is whoever sleeps there. The <strong>booker</strong> is whoever made the booking — often the same person, but not with a company or a travel agency. The <strong>company</strong> is the legal entity that gets the invoice.
103|Search works per word: type as many as you like, in any order, and every word has to match something on the profile. "maria smit" and "smit maria" both find Maria Smit. It starts searching from two characters, and it answers with at most a hundred matches at a time — see that limit and your search is too broad, not your file too small.
104|Every profile has a free notes field. That is a memory aid for whoever opens the profile, not a way to select a group of guests: it is not searched and there are no labels.
105|Price agreements
106|A <em>fixed rate</em> for a company or a regular guest
107|A booker and a company can both carry a price agreement: a discount percentage or a fixed nightly rate, as one agreement for everything or per room type. That is what gives a corporate client their negotiated rate by itself, without reception having to remember it or retype it.
108|<strong>If the booker is linked to a company and both have an agreement, the company's wins. Completely.</strong> Also for a room type the company left blank and the booker did not. That is deliberate: one change on the company has to reach every booker under it. If the booker's own agreement could still slip in, every rate change would mean checking all its bookers one by one.
109|A new reservation gets the rate straight away. On an existing one you are asked first whether the prices should be recalculated — otherwise a booking that has already been discussed would quietly change amount.
110|Your own booking page
111|A corporate client who books <em>themselves</em> at their rate
112|A booker can unlock their agreed rate on your own booking page with no account and no password: their email address plus the booker code from their profile is the whole sign-in. It is a lookup, not a session — it holds for that one booking and nothing is remembered afterwards.
113|The discount is worked out again on the server, for the payment and for the reservation that is written. Nothing comes from the guest's browser, so an agreement cannot be claimed by someone who does not have one.
114|Three consequences: a promo code does not combine with a negotiated rate — the agreement wins and the code is refused. Remove the agreement while somebody is mid-booking and that booking is refused with an invitation to start again, rather than quietly going through at the public price. And the sign-in fills in only the name and the email address typed: the rest of the profile stays on the server, because a booker code is short and therefore guessable.
115|Duplicates
116|The same guest is in there <em>four times</em>
117|That is normal, not a malfunction. The main cause: OTAs never give you your guest's real address. Booking.com and Airbnb send a fresh relay address with every booking, so a returning guest arrives looking like a stranger every time.
118|rumo spots those duplicates itself and shows one card per person, not per combination: four profiles of the same Maria Smit are one card saying "Maria Smit · 4 profiles", with the fields that tell them apart. So the counter counts people.
119|<strong>Nothing is ever merged by itself.</strong> Two real people can share a name. Merge them yourself and the reservations and invoices move to the profile you keep while the others disappear; anything already checked in keeps the name recorded at the time, because that is the legal register. A merge cannot be undone, so read the confirmation.
120|Groups &amp; <em>allotments</em>
121|Holding rooms for a conference, a company or a travel agency. What you hold, what comes back, and who gets the bill.
122|Setting up
123|Recording a <em>block of rooms</em>
124|An allotment holds rooms aside for one party. You record which room type it is and how many of them you hold, between which dates, at which rate, and by when the agreement stops. That last one is the cut-off.
125|Everyone with the page may read: admin, manager and reception. <strong>Creating, changing and deleting is admin and manager.</strong> A receptionist sees the whole picture — the blocks, the pickup, the billing — but does not change the contract. That is enforced on the server, so it holds however you reach the screen.
126|Availability
127|What an allotment does to your <em>availability</em>
128|Rooms you hold come off what goes out to the channels <em>and</em> off what your own booking page still offers. Hold five twins with two booked out of the block, and three stay withheld on Booking.com, on the other channels and on your own site.
129|Three conditions have to hold: the allotment is active, the rooms have not been released yet, and the day falls inside the stay dates. An allotment without dates withholds nothing. Once the block is fully booked it withholds nothing either, because there is nothing left to hold.
130|One exception: whoever comes through the group's own booking link may use the block up. That is what it is being held for.
131|Cut-off
132|What happens at the <em>cut-off</em>
133|The cut-off is the moment the agreement stops: whatever the group has not booked by then goes back on general sale. Once a day, in the early hours, rumo looks for allotments whose cut-off has passed and that have not been released, and releases them. The rooms reappear in what goes out to the channels, and every release lands in the history.
134|The cut-off day itself is still yours: the bell shows those allotments so a manager can release earlier. Releasing is one-way — it is recorded once, and a second pass changes nothing.
135|One invoice for the <em>company</em>, or one per guest
136|An allotment bills in one of two ways. <strong>Individual</strong> is the default: every reservation gets its own invoice, like any other booking. <strong>Central</strong> makes one invoice for the whole contract, in the company's name.
137|On that central invoice go the rooms of every linked reservation (except cancelled ones and no-shows), the extras marked as centrally billed on their reservation, and the city tax only if you set the scope to everything. That is how you send the room to the company and the minibar to the guest. Every line keeps its own detail — room number, guest name, room type, rate, dates and booking reference — so the company can check the invoice against who slept where.
138|Watch the payment terms: on central billing they are <em>not</em> enforced. The company is invoiced afterwards, so there is nothing to collect at booking time. Set <em>prepaid</em> there and it stays what you typed and does nothing. If you want money up front from a company, that is an invoice you send and follow up.
139|Housekeeping &amp; <em>room status</em>
140|The bridge between what the guest sees and what the team does. The status of every room, the cleaning schedule, blocks for maintenance. All on one screen, usable on a tablet in the corridor too.
141|Status
142|The four <em>room statuses</em>
143|Four core statuses, not fifteen.
144|<strong>Dirty:</strong> to be cleaned, after a check-out or on request during a stay
145|<strong>Clean:</strong> ready for the next guest
146|<strong>In-house:</strong> occupied by a guest
147|<strong>Blocked:</strong> not available (maintenance, own use)
148|rumo sets the status to dirty automatically on every check-out. Housekeeping marks clean from the mobile view in the corridor, no separate app.
149|Planning
150|A <em>cleaning list</em> for the team
151|Every morning rumo generates a cleaning list based on check-outs, stay-overs and special requests (extra towels, allergy pillows, early check-in). You do not set the order yourself: the list falls into tabs — departures, stayovers, arrivals and vacant rooms — and that is where the ranking comes from.
152|The list can be printed or shown on a tablet. Tickable while the team works, with progress visible live at the desk. That way you know which rooms you can give your early guests without ringing first.
153|Add-on
154|What <em>Advanced Housekeeping</em> adds
155|Without the add-on a room has four statuses. With the add-on <strong>inspected</strong> is added, between clean and ready for the guest. That way nobody checks anyone into a room that has not been looked over.
156|You also get cleaning checklists that can differ per day, separate extra tasks and a note per room, and a timer that measures how long a clean really takes. That last one comes back in a time report per person and per room.
157|Switch the add-on off and the basics keep working: clean and dirty do fine without it.
158|Blocks
159|Blocking a room for <em>maintenance</em>
160|Drag a block on the calendar, choose <em>block</em>, and give a reason (leak, renovation, own use). The room is taken out of the pool for OTA sale during that period. You see the block as a striped zone on the calendar.
161|You block a room yourself on the calendar. Rudy does not line this up for you, but he can explain in plain language where you have to be and what happens then.
162|Breakfast &amp; <em>F&amp;B</em>
163|How many covers there are tomorrow, which rooms they belong to, and what the kitchen needs to know about each of them. An overview, not a till.
164|The count
165|Which day does a <em>meal</em> belong to?
166|This is the rule that surprises people, and it follows the stay, not the calendar. <strong>Breakfast is the morning after a night:</strong> whoever arrives today has breakfast tomorrow, whoever leaves tomorrow still has one that morning. <strong>Dinner is the evening of a night,</strong> so whoever leaves gets none. <strong>Lunch is only for those in house:</strong> not on the arrival day and not on the departure day.
167|One cover is one person, one meal, one day. If the meal sits in the rate plan, every guest in the room counts every day. If it is an extra, the booked quantity is spread over the right days: a breakfast for two of the four mornings occupies exactly those two.
168|There are three meals: breakfast, lunch and dinner. Which of them your hotel runs you set in the settings. At least one is always on — switching the last one off does not work and falls back to breakfast.
169|Allergies
170|What the kitchen <em>has to know</em> about a room
171|Every room on a reservation can carry F&amp;B labels and a free note. There is no separate diet or allergy field: an allergy is recorded as a label or a note, and it shows up in red on the kitchen list and on the print-out.
172|A catalogue item can also be marked as a <em>meal note</em> rather than a meal. That gives the kitchen a line ("late breakfast", "no pork") without adding a cover. A room with only notes is on the list all the same, with zero covers.
173|Ticking off who has been is possible, and two people working the same service see each other's ticks appear without refreshing. It is an aid for the floor and nothing more: it changes no counts, it is not billed and it does not reach the report.
174|Walk-ins
175|Somebody sits down <em>unexpectedly</em>
176|A walk-in here is a guest who is in house and had not booked that meal. You add one from that meal's own tab: pick the room, the number of people, done. It lands as an ordinary billable extra on their reservation, for that one day, and a mistake can be undone straight away.
177|Two limits. Rooms that already have the meal are not offered, so you cannot double-book anyone. And a restaurant guest who is not staying at the hotel cannot be recorded here: every walk-in hangs on a booking.
178|The price comes from the first catalogue item ticked for that meal. If that item does not exist, the walk-in cannot be added at all.
179|Door codes &amp; <em>keys</em>
180|A numeric code instead of a card. When the guest gets it, what reception sees, and what happens when the network drops. Paid add-on.
181|The idea
182|One code per <em>room</em>, not per guest
183|Each room has one fixed identity in the lock system, and that carries that room's key. rumo never creates or deletes those identities; it only changes the code on them. A guest arrives and the room's code becomes their code. They leave and it is replaced by one nobody knows, which locks them out immediately.
184|Two consequences. There is <strong>one code per room at a time</strong>, so two guests of the same booking with two rooms have two different codes. And the lock's own log says "room 101", not the guest's name — rumo knows who held the code at any moment, the lock does not.
185|This is the <strong>Key Management</strong> add-on, not a standard part. Without it there is no key icon on a reservation, no tab in the settings and no card in the guest portal. Today rumo works with Salto KS; Dormakaba is announced but not connected yet.
186|Timing
187|When the guest <em>gets</em> their code
188|The code appears when two things are true: the previous guest has been checked out, and housekeeping has set the room to clean or inspected. Not before — a code that exists earlier opens a door with somebody still behind it.
189|On top of that you can require the guest to check in online first, to pay their balance, or both. Those are guest-portal settings, separate from the settings for checking in and paying themselves. Until then the portal explains what is missing.
190|The code is <strong>never sent by email</strong>. An email cannot be taken back and sits in a mailbox for years; a portal link can be withdrawn and the card can close again.
191|At the desk
192|What <em>reception</em> sees and can do
193|On a reservation, every room row has a key icon that opens the door-code panel. The code is there in full, with no extra click — somebody with a guest on the phone should not have to hunt for six digits. If there is no code yet, it says why: the room is still held by another booking (named), the room is being prepared, the arrival day has not come, or the lock system did not answer.
194|There is a button for a new code. It replaces that room's code immediately, for everyone who has it, even if the guest is already inside — so the guest has to be told the new one. It only works from the booking that currently holds the code; from another reservation it is refused, otherwise you would quietly change the code of whoever is actually in the room.
195|Opening a door remotely is possible too, and that is recorded in rumo with the name of whoever pressed the button. The lock system itself only ever sees "rumo".
196|When it fails
197|No network, no <em>new</em> codes
198|A digital key is not a replacement for a way in that needs no electricity. Keep a set of tags at reception and keep the mechanical override your installer supplied within reach.
199|If the connection is gone for longer, the codes issued in the meantime never reached the locks. Issue them again afterwards or hand out tags.
200|The button in the portal that opens a door needs the phone, rumo, the vendor and the gateway all at once, so it is the first thing to fail. That is why the card always says "not working? use your code" — and if the code does not work either, it is the desk.
201|Channel <em>manager</em>
202|How rumo and the OTAs talk to each other, so that one change at your end goes neatly to Booking, Expedia, Airbnb and the rest. Below is what you need to know about it.
203|How it works
204|What goes <em>back and forth</em>
205|Out to the channels go your prices, your availability, your restrictions (min-stay, max-stay, closed on arrival or departure) and the link between your room types and the channel's. Back come new bookings and cancellations, as soon as the OTA reports them.
206|In practice: you change a price in rumo and within seconds it is on every connected channel. A new booking on Booking.com is in your calendar just as fast. Every change has its own status line: sent, forwarded, arrived.
207|<em>Connecting</em> a new OTA
208|For every channel there is a fixed path: allow rumo as a provider in the OTA's extranet, pass on your property ID, and then link your room types to the channel's in rumo. With Airbnb that goes through a login connection, with Booking through an agreement they have to activate.
209|We do the first connection <strong>together</strong>. From the second on it usually goes by itself. Rudy points out the steps, but choices in an OTA's extranet (a Genius promo, a Mobile Rate, your listing text) he leaves to you: those are decisions, not actions.
210|Sync
211|When is a change visible at the OTA?
212|Usually within <em>a few seconds</em>. In busy periods it can take half a minute. If it takes longer, it is down to the channel itself, and you see that in your status log. If something is stuck, you can push it again by hand.
213|One important nuance: <strong>cancellations, no-shows and price changes after booking</strong> you do not send back to the OTA from rumo. Most OTAs simply do not accept that type of change. You do it in their extranet, and the status then comes back to rumo by itself.
214|Commission
215|Why you get no <em>commission invoice</em> from Expedia
216|Booking.com is a broker: the guest is your customer and the commission comes afterwards as an invoice. Expedia Collect is a reseller: it keeps its share before the money reaches you, so there is never a commission invoice and there is nothing to deduct.
217|Put your fixed percentage on that anyway and you deduct that same commission a second time, and the channel looks more expensive than it is.

219|Conflicts
220|A double booking, now what?
221|Theoretically impossible thanks to real-time sync, but in practice it happens once in a while (two OTAs selling the last room at the same time). rumo warns you immediately. You decide which guest you upgrade and which you move, not the algorithms.
222|In the pipeline
223|Per OTA: Booking.com, Airbnb, Expedia
224|Rates &amp; <em>packages</em>
225|One base rate, and the rest is derived. No entering four different rate plans by hand for one room. No 23 screens to set a weekend price.
226|Base model
227|How rumo models prices
228|You start from a <em>base rate</em> per room type. On top come modifiers: occupancy (1, 2, 3 people), child price, length of stay, channel uplift, season. You adjust the modifier, not the four derived rates.
229|That is different from most channel managers, where you have to maintain a separate line for every occupancy. We think that is unnecessary work.
230|Occupancy
231|Rates per occupancy (1, 2, 3 people)
232|For a room where 1, 2 or 3 people can stay: set one base rate and two modifiers. For example <em>−€30 for single occupancy</em> and <em>+€40 for the third guest</em>. rumo works out the three rates and sends them all to the OTAs.
233|Packages
234|Building a <em>package</em> (room + breakfast + dinner)
235|A package is a rate with extras included. "Romantic weekend" = room + breakfast + four-course menu, for one price. On the invoice the three items are split out neatly (handy for VAT); on the OTA rate guests see one amount.
236|Periods
237|Season and weekend prices
238|Select a date range on the calendar, choose the modifier ("+15% for the summer season"), done. Works on all room types at once or on one. Can also be per weekday: a different price on Friday/Saturday than on Monday/Tuesday.
239|Worked example
240|How a price on <em>Booking.com</em> comes about
241|Concretely, with figures. Junior Suite, base rate €140 per night for 2 people. A guest books 2 nights Saturday-Sunday, with 3 people, through Booking.com:
242|Base rate: <strong>€140</strong>
243|+ Weekend (+10%): <strong>€154</strong>
244|+ Third person (+€40): <strong>€194</strong>
245|+ OTA uplift Booking (+12%): <strong>€217</strong>
246|× 2 nights: <strong>€434</strong>
